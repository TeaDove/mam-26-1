## HISTORICAL SURVEY

Controlled rolling is a means of improving the properties of steel in the hot-rolled condition to a level equivalent to, or greater than those of highly alloyed or heat-treated steels. Modern controlled rolling has been practised on numerous mill and laboratory experiments which have been performed to obtain high strength, good toughness, and good weldability. The basic idea is to control the rolling process in order to refine the austenite structure, and thereby to give fine ferrite grains.

In 1924 Arrowsmith had already pointed out the advantage of grain refinement. It has been known for many years that improvements in mechanical properties result when structural steels are rolled to low finishing temperatures. Hanemann and Lucke interpreted this in terms of recrystallization after hot deformation with the α-grain (ferrite grain) size being reduced with decreasing deformation temperature in the γ-region (austenite region). In 1947 Barr and Tipper showed that in mild steel plates the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature was raised by an increase in α-grain size. Examining as-rolled plates, Mackenzie showed a correlation between transition temperature and finish-rolling temperature, a factor affecting γ-grain size. Similar results have been reported by other investigators. The above reports have one factor in common: hot rolling was carried out as usual at high temperatures, and only the last one or two passes were rolled at a lower temperature. Therefore, the results obtained were not always definite, and were in disagreement with each other.

After World War 2, in order to prevent the occurrence of brittle fracture, notch toughness was required in shipbuilding and in other structural steel plates. In general, the refinements of microstructure by Al (aluminium) killing, normalizing, and an increase in the ratio of manganese to carbon content were adopted to meet the requirement for notch toughness. Instead of normalizing, however, controlled low-temperature hot rolling was being practised by some mills in Europe to provide increased notch toughness in hot-rolled plate. The practice involved making the last few hot-rolling passes at temperatures lower than normal. The optimum temperature for the last pass was just above the Ar3 temperature. This rolling schedule was fairly similar to the present controlled rolling and its prototype.

In 1959, Beiser reported that the small addition of Nb (niobium) caused a large increase in strength but reduced notch toughness markedly in as-hot-rolled steel plates. It was Great Lakes Steel, which produced as-hot-rolled high-strength Nb-bearing steels for the first time in 1960. In order to prevent a deterioration in notch toughness, the application of controlled rolling to Nb-bearing steel was studied. According to Norén's review:

(i) when hot rolled under conventional rolling conditions, Nb steel deteriorated markedly in toughness; (ii) when finish rolled at lower temperatures, the improvement in toughness was much greater for Nb steel than for Nb-free steel; and (iii) when finish rolled below 800 °C, or when total reduction below 900 °C was more than 30%, notch toughness improved remarkably. The strength increase resulting from the addition of Nb was found to be due to precipitation hardening of fine Nb(C, N) which formed in the α-matrix during cooling. In order to make full use of precipitation hardening, Nb(C, N) had to be dissolved at a high temperature before hot rolling, which coarsened the γ-grain structure. When coarse γ was hot rolled conventionally, the transformed structure was a mixture consisting of coarse α and bainite, thereby deteriorating notch toughness. Using these methods, the basis of combining controlled rolling with Nb steel was established.
