## FUNDAMENTALS OF CONTROLLED ROLLING

The major purpose of controlled rolling is to refine the structure of steel and, thereby, to enhance both strength and toughness. Because of the relation between γ– and α-grain (ferrite grain) sizes, refinement of the α-structure is achieved mainly through γ-grain (austenite grain) refinement. The change in grain structure with deformation temperature has been studied by many investigators. Many studies were confined to deformation in the γ-region alone, while few of them were extended to deformation in the γ–α (austenite–ferrite) two-phase region.

Kozasu et al. divided deformation temperatures in the γ-region into three ranges in association with the change in γ- and α-grain structures. Deformation above 1000 °C brings about coarse, recrystallized γ-grains, which transform to a relatively coarse α and upper bainitic structure (range 1). Deformation in the intermediate temperature range from 1000 °C to 900 °C refines γ by repeated recrystallization, leading to fine-grained α (range 2). Deformation below the recrystallization temperature produces 'warm-worked' γ, which leads to a finer α-grain structure (range 3). The grain refinement is achieved mainly by deformation in ranges 2 and 3. The transition temperature was shown to decrease in a linear manner with an increase in total reduction in ranges 2 and 3.

From a practical viewpoint, Baird and Preston proposed a similar but slightly different interpretation. Figure 1 depicts the rolling procedures involving reduction in the rapid-recrystallization region (range 1), delay in rolling (in range 2), and final reduction in the non-recrystallization region (range 3). Delay in rolling is a necessary procedure for ensuring the required amount of deformation in the non-recrystallization region. During the delay, however, partial recrystallization tends to occur, thereby leading to the formation of a mixed grain structure.
