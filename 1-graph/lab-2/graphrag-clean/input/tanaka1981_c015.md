## PROPERTIES OF CONTROLLED-ROLLED STEEL

## Anisotropy due to development of texture

When compared in term of the same carbon equivalent, controlled-rolled steel has greater strength than the conventionally hot-rolled type, and it exhibits a lower transition temperature than normalized, or quenched and tempered steel. However, controlled-rolled steel has its own disadvantages. The most serious one is the anisotropy in mechanical properties due to texture development.

In controlled-rolled steel a crystallographic texture develops, the magnitude of which depends on whether deformation is carried out in the non-recrystallization region or in the two-phase region, or whether the microstructure consists of α (ferrite) or bainite. Yutori and Ogawa reported that even when deformation in the non-recrystallization region is given under the same rolling condition, the intensity in texture is different depending on transformed microstructure, see Fig. 24. All three structure exhibit a similar texture, consisting of $\langle 110\rangle \| \mathrm{RD}$ (rolling direction) orientation with the strongest component of $\{113\} \langle 110\rangle$ and $\{332\} \langle 113\rangle$. However, there is a remarkable difference in intensity between polygonal ferrite-pearlite and acicular ferrite or martensite. When steel has a low Mn (manganese) and/or Ni content, producing an α-structure, it shows a weak texture intensity. However, with an increasing Mn and/or Ni content the microstructure becomes bainitic, developing a texture with $\{332\} \langle 113\rangle$ and $\{311\} \langle 011\rangle$ orientation.
