## Separation

In controlled-rolled steel, a matter more serious than plane anisotropy is embrittlement in the through-thickness direction, resulting mainly from the formation of a $\{100\}$ texture. Figure 29 shows schematically the full Charpy curves and number of separation in the L(longitudinal) and Z(through-thickness) direction, where curves of the T(transverse) direction are located near those of the L direction. The difference in transition temperature between the L and Z direction is more than 100 K. The low transition temperature in the L direction is due to separation (very often called delamination or splitting) which occur in a brittle manner in a fractured Charpy specimen parallel to the plate surface. As shown in Fig. 30, separation is greatest near the temperature where the ductile-fracture area becomes just 100%, being nil in the brittle range or at high temperature. In contrast to conventionally hot-rolled steel, the Charpy impact energy of controlled-rolled steel increases with increasing temperature even in the ductile-fraction region, while the number of separation decreases. Impact energy reaches the shelf energy and separation disappears when the temperature reaches the transition temperature in the Z direction.

The occurrence of separation, accompanied by a lowering of the transition temperature, has been explained in term of the critical stress criterion. When the Charpy specimen is given a bending force the maximum stress are induced at the plastic/elastic interface, just beneath a notch under the plane-strain condition. Induced stress is greatest in the $L$ direction, followed by the $Z$ and $T$ direction, when a longitudinal specimen is considered. The condition for the occurrence of separation is given by

$$
\sigma_ {\mathrm{f}} ^ {\mathrm{L}} > \sigma_ {\mathrm{L}} \quad , \tag{5}
$$

$$
\sigma_ {\mathrm{f}} ^ {\mathrm{Z}} = \sigma_ {\mathrm{Z}} \quad , \tag{6}
$$

where $\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}^{\mathrm{L}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}^{\mathrm{Z}}$ are the cleavage fracture strengths in the $L$ and $Z$ direction, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{Z}}$ are the stress acting in the $L$ and $Z$ direction, respectively. By subtracting (6) from (5), we obtain

$$
\sigma_ {\mathrm{f}} ^ {\mathrm{L}} - \sigma_ {\mathrm{f}} ^ {\mathrm{Z}} \geqslant \sigma_ {\mathrm{L}} - \sigma_ {\mathrm{Z}} = k ^ {\prime} \sigma_ {\mathrm{y}} \tag{7}
$$

where $\sigma_{y}$ is the uniaxial yield stress and $k'$ is a constant. As is clear from equation (7), separation occurs only when there is anisotropy in the fracture strength, i.e. only when fracture strength is lower for the Z direction than for the L direction. As the occurrence of separation changes the stress state from plane strain to plane stress, it becomes more difficult for brittle fracture to occur. Embury et al. and Miyoshi et al. reported that separated material behaves in a similar manner to that of laminated plate.
