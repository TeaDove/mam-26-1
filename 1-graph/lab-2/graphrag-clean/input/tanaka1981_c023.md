The cause of through-thickness embrittlement has been studied by many investigators, and the following suggested: (i) a crack initiates at an MnS inclusion flattened by low-temperature finish rolling, and propagates along grains with $\{100\}$ orientation and/or a banded structure; (ii) the clusters of crystal grains with $\{100\}$ orientation are the main sources of separation cracks, since separation occurs even in steel with an extremely low S content, and this is strongly supported by the observation that separation increased with increasing intensity in the $\{100\}$ texture; (iii) intergranular failure along prior γ-boundaries (austenite boundaries), probably embrittled by the segregation of impurities, increases the occurrence of separations; (iv) in hot-rolled strip, temper embrittlement caused by the segregation of P to α-grain (ferrite grain) boundaries in the rich layer during slow cooling after coiling deteriorates boundary toughness; (v) dimensional anisotropy of the microstructure produced by controlled rolling — γ-grain in the case of the intergranular fracture, and α-grain in the case of transgranular — is the main cause of through-thickness embrittlement; (vi) pearlite and/or a bainite banded structure; and (vii) a combination of these effects.

The mechanism initiating separation may depend on chemical composition, microstructure, and hot-rolling condition. When stress is induced in the through-thickness direction, cracks initiate at the place where the fracture strength is lowest, and propagate along the second weakest location. When flattened MnS exists in steel, it triggers the initiation of the crack, which propagates along grains with $\{100\}$ orientation and/or bainite and/or the pearlite band, depending on the situation. If MnS exists very occasionally in steel with an extremely low S content, separation will initiate at the second weakest location.

Separation has a large effect on transition temperature and absorbed energy. The latter is influenced most markedly near the temperature where shear area becomes just 100%, exhibiting the greatest number of separations. As shown in Fig. 31, both absorbed energy and transition temperature decrease with an increasing number of separations. As is clear from the above explanation, the low-temperature toughness in the L and T directions is improved by the sacrifice of that in the Z direction.
