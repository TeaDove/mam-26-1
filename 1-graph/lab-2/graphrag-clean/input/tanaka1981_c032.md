## Acicular ferrite steel

Acicular ferrite, HSLA (high-strength low-alloy) steel has been developed by the Climax Molybdenum Company. Low-carbon ($\leqslant$0.06%) Mn (manganese)–Mo (molybdenum)–Nb (niobium) steel which will transform to upper bainite from undeformed or conventionally hot-rolled γ (austenite), transforms to acicular ferrite, when transformation is accelerated by controlled rolling. Figure 37 shows the acicular ferrite structure (Fig. 37a) as well as the bainitic structure (Fig. 37b). The acicular ferrite structure consists of fine non-equiaxed ferrite dispersed with cementite and martensite islands.

The acicular ferrite is defined as a highly substructured, non-equiaxed phase that forms on continuous cooling by a mixed diffusion and shear mode of transformation at a temperature slightly higher than the upper bainite transformation range. Acicular ferrite is different from bainitic ferrite with respect to the prior γ-grain-boundary network and cementite and martensite morphologies; in the bainitic structure, the prior γ-boundary network is retained and plate-like cementites are formed at coarse lath boundaries as well as the prior grain boundaries, whereas in the acicular ferrite structure the prior γ-grain-boundary network is completely eliminated having a fine-grained structure, and small cementite and martensite islands are scattered throughout the matrix (see Fig. 37). Because of the above difference in microstructure between bainitic and acicular ferrite there is a distinct difference in the facet unit (effective grain size) which, in turn, causes a large difference in transition temperature between the two. In the bainitic structure, a facet unit is nearly equal to the prior γ-grain size, whereas in the acicular ferrite a facet unit is much smaller than the prior γ-grain size, giving a low transition temperature.

The fully acicular ferrite which, being free of polygonal ferrite, contains a small amount of cementite and martensite, exhibits high strength but poor toughness. A significant improvement in toughness can be obtained in the mixed structure of acicular plus fine-grained polygonal ferrite by lowering the finish-rolling temperature and by increasing the amount of deformation in the non-recrystallization region. The reason for the improvement is grain refinement in the polygonal ferrite as well as acicular ferrite. As shown in Fig. 38, with the increase in the amount of deformation in the non-recrystallization region, fine-grained polygonal ferrite increases in volume fraction at the expense of acicular ferrite which, in turn, causes an improvement in strength and low-temperature toughness. The optimum combination of strength and toughness is obtained with ~15% of polygonal ferrite. On the other hand, the introduction of coarse polygonal ferrite to acicular ferrite causes a deterioration in strength properties as well as impact properties. Causes of coarse polygonal-ferrite formation are insufficient hardenability and inadequate controlled rolling in terms of finish-rolling temperature, and the amount of deformation in the non-recrystallization region.

Another feature of acicular ferrite steel is continuous yielding followed by rapid work hardening, which is paramount to offsetting any loss in yield strength due to the Bauschinger effect. The Bauschinger effect has a pronounced effect on discontinuous yielding, polygonal steels.
