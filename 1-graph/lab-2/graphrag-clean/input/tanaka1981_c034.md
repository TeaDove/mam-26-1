## Controlled rolling as pretreatment

By making use of grain-refining effect, controlled rolling is employed as a pretreatment before normalizing or quenching and tempering. This is based on the fact that a fine-grained structure produced by controlled rolling persists even in the reheated condition, probably due to the dispersion of fine Nb(C, N) precipitates. In Nb (niobium) and 3.5%Ni steels, controlled rolling plus normalizing produces higher strengths and better toughness than normalizing alone.

Linepipe steel for Arctic use is usually produced by controlled rolling. However, when the thickness is increased too much the controlled-rolling effect cannot be exerted over the whole thickness. Controlled rolling plus quenching and tempering produces much tougher material than the single process alone. The fine γ-grain (austenite grain) size before quenching is responsible for this. Controlled rolling plus controlled cooling and controlled rolling followed by direct quenching have been tried.

A new type of thermomechanical treatment has been proposed by Miyoshi et al. After controlled rolling, C-Mn and Nb steels are cooled below the Ar1 temperature and subsequently heated to ~900 °C, which produces a very fine-grained γ-structure owing to the superimposed effects of controlled rolling, transformation, and normalizing. Fine-grained γ is once again controlled rolled, producing an extremely fine and uniform α-grain (ferrite grain) structure.

## Continuum rolling

So far, controlled rolling in the γ–α (austenite–ferrite) two-phase and/or α regions has been described. Melloy and Dennison proposed continuum rolling, a unique thermomechanical process involving reductions of γ,γ+α, and α. Figure 39 shows that with continuum rolling the yield strength increases, while the transition temperature decreases with a decrease in finishing temperature in the α-region. Melloy and Dennison attributed this desirable combination of increased strength and toughness to (i) grain refinement of both γ and α by heavy, repeated deformations; (ii) a dynamic recovery that produces a fine polygonal substructure; and (iii) cube-on-corner crystallographic texturing that results from the deformation process.

The results were obtained in extra-low-carbon steel but whether or not the same results will be obtained in low-alloy steels remains unsolved. It is also difficult to apply this process for practical use.
